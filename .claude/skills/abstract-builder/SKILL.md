# Abstract builder

Most readers see only the title and abstract, and so do editors deciding on desk rejects and conference organizers filling sessions. An abstract has one job: tell a reader in the right subfield what was learned and why it changes what they thought.

## Step 1: get the parts

From the draft, notes, or conversation, extract:
- **The puzzle or problem**: what the field assumes or can't explain.
- **The claim**: the finding or argument, in one sentence.
- **Evidence**: data, sample, method — with N and setting.
- **The contribution**: what changes in the literature if this is right. Name the literature.

If the claim can't be written in one sentence, the abstract won't work; help the user find the sentence first. This is the most useful thing the skill does.

## Step 2: check the venue

Word limits and norms differ:
- Journal abstracts: usually 150–250 words (check guidelines; *ASR* ~200, many Elsevier journals ~250 with required keywords and "highlights").
- ASA annual meeting: short abstract plus a longer extended abstract or full paper, depending on session type — check the current call.
- PAA extended abstracts: ~2 pages with preliminary results.
- Fellowships (NSF GRFP, Ford): intellectual merit and broader impacts framing.

Ask which venue only if unclear; otherwise infer and state it.

## Step 3: draft

Structure for empirical papers, about one to two sentences each:
1. Problem/puzzle, in the literature's terms.
2. What this paper does.
3. Data and method (concrete: "Using 2000–2019 AFCARS data on 1.2 million foster care exits…").
4. The key finding(s) with direction and, where possible, magnitude.
5. The contribution — what it means for theory or policy.

For theoretical or historical papers (e.g., genealogical work), replace 3–4 with the sources/approach and the argument's main moves.

Write the finding as a finding ("privatization widened rural–urban gaps in permanency") rather than a promise ("we examine the relationship between…"). "We examine" abstracts hide the result and are the most common weak pattern.

## Step 4: title and keywords

Offer 3 title options: one descriptive, one with a colon-subtitle hook, one leading with the finding. Keywords: 4–6, mixing the substantive area, the mechanism, and the method; avoid repeating title words, since search already indexes those.

## Step 5: check

Count words against the limit. Check every number against the paper. Remove jargon a neighboring subfield wouldn't know. Read the first sentence alone — it should make an editor in the target journal want the second.

Deliver the abstract with its word count, the title options, keywords, and one line on what was cut, if anything, and why.
