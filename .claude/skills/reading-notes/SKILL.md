# Reading notes

Notes for graduate seminars and comprehensive exams have two jobs: let the reader reconstruct the argument months later without rereading, and connect the reading to the reader's own intellectual project. Summaries do the first badly and the second not at all.

## Before writing

Read the whole text you were given. If you only have a citation, say whether you are working from the full text, a partial text, or general knowledge of the work — and if general knowledge, say so at the top of the notes, because quoted definitions and page numbers cannot be trusted without the text.

Identify the genre, because it changes what matters:
- **Theory** (Bourdieu, Foucault, Du Bois, Crenshaw): the concepts and what problem they were built to solve.
- **Empirical article**: the question, design, data, key finding, and whether the design supports the claim.
- **Review or programmatic essay**: the map of the field it draws and what it argues should come next.

## Structure

```
# <Author (Year). Title.>

## The argument in three sentences
  What problem, what claim, what it replaces or opposes.

## Key concepts
  **Concept** — definition in the author's words where possible (quote with page number),
  then one line in plain language. Note what the concept is designed to do.

## How the argument proceeds
  Short outline by section/chapter. Enough to find things again.

## Evidence and method
  What kind of support the argument rests on. For empirical work: data, sample, design,
  and one honest sentence on what it can and cannot show.

## In conversation with
  Who the author is answering, building on, or contradicting — and thinkers the user already
  works with who meet this reading (lineage, alliance, tension, or a shared object).

## For my work
  Concrete uses: a concept that names something in the user's project, a mechanism to test,
  a critique the user must answer, a citation for a specific claim.

## Critiques
  The strongest objections, including ones from the user's own theoretical commitments.

## Discussion questions
  3–5 questions that would actually start a seminar argument, not comprehension checks.

## Quotes worth keeping
  Verbatim, with page numbers.
```

## Making the connections real

The user is a sociology PhD student working on stratification and mobility, child welfare, postsecondary education, critical race theory, and epistemology, and keeps an influences map relating thinkers by lineage, alliance, tension, and shared object (Marx, Weber, Bourdieu, Wacquant, Foucault, Du Bois, Bell, Crenshaw, Collins, Roberts, Gilmore, Tilly, Wright, and others). When the user's repository is available, `influences.html` lists them. Use those four relation types in "In conversation with" — it keeps the notes consistent with how the user already thinks about the field.

Don't force connections. One precise link ("Roberts's claim about surveillance of poor parents is an empirical instance of Foucault's disciplinary gaze, applied to the family") beats five vague ones.

## Accuracy

Quote only what is in the text you have. Never invent page numbers. If translating concepts across editions or translations (common with Bourdieu, Foucault, Weber), note which translation the quotes come from.
