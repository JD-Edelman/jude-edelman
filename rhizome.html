Theoretical Inspirations

This page is a map rather than a lineage. It has no root and no first entry — each visit opens somewhere different, in the middle of things. Follow a line to see what it connects; cut one to see what grows in its place.

“Any point of a rhizome can be connected to anything other, and must be.” — Deleuze & Guattari, A Thousand Plateaus

The Plateaus

  • Friedrich Nietzsche

    On the Genealogy of Morality (1887)

    Genealogy as a method: values have a history, and that history is one of struggle rather than discovery. Asking what a moral category was for, and whom it served, before asking whether it is true.

  • G. W. F. Hegel

    Phenomenology of Spirit (1807)

    The master–slave dialectic: consciousness comes to itself only through another, and domination deforms the dominator as well. The figure that everything downstream — Marx, Beauvoir, Fanon — argues with.

  • Karl Marx

    Capital (1867)

    Social forms as historical products of production rather than natural facts, and the trick by which a relation between people comes to look like a property of things. The vocabulary for asking where the capital in an institution comes from.

  • Max Weber

    Economy and Society (1922)

    Bureaucracy, legitimacy, and the rationalization that makes a decision feel like a procedure. Status and party alongside class, which is what it takes to describe institutions that sort people on more than one axis at once.

  • Gaston Bachelard

    The Formation of the Scientific Mind (1938)

    The epistemological break, and the obstacles that common sense puts in the way of knowing. A scientific fact is won against appearances rather than collected from them.

  • Pierre Bourdieu

    The Craft of Sociology (1968); Distinction (1979)

    Capital in its economic, social, and cultural forms, converting into one another as it passes through institutions. Symbolic violence names the moment a contingent sorting comes to feel like a description of what people simply are.

  • Michel Foucault

    Discipline and Punish (1975)

    Power as productive rather than merely prohibitive: it makes categories, records, and kinds of people rather than only forbidding things. The claim that institutions produce the populations they claim to merely sort begins here.

  • Loïc Wacquant

    Urban Outcasts (2008); Punishing the Poor (2009)

    The welfare and penal arms of the state as a single apparatus for managing poverty, one hand supervising what the other withdraws. Where Bourdieu's field theory meets the administration of the poor.

  • Gilles Deleuze

    Nietzsche and Philosophy (1962); Difference and Repetition (1968)

    Difference as prior to identity rather than a falling-away from it. The rhizome against the tree — knowledge as a burrow with many entrances — is the form of this page.

  • Félix Guattari

    The Three Ecologies (1989); Chaosmosis (1992)

    The clinician of the pair, working at La Borde on the question of how institutions themselves fall ill. Schizoanalysis against the family drama: desire as something produced by arrangements rather than by a private wound.

  • Jacques Derrida

    Of Grammatology (1967); “Cogito and the History of Madness” (1963)

    Meaning as deferred and differential, never fully present in a term. The binaries an institution runs on — fit and unfit, placement and removal — hold their shape only by suppressing what they exclude.

  • Jean-François Lyotard

    The Postmodern Condition (1979); The Differend (1983)

    The differend: a wrong that cannot be phrased in the idiom of the tribunal that would judge it, so that presenting the harm and being heard become mutually exclusive. The condition of anyone whose case survives only in the language of the agency that made it.

  • Simone de Beauvoir

    The Second Sex (1949)

    One is not born but becomes: a category lived as destiny is shown to be made. Her reading of Hegel's Other is the hinge between the dialectic and every later account of how the sorted come to see themselves through the sorting.

  • Carl Jung

    Psychological Types (1921); The Archetypes and the Collective Unconscious (1959)

    The unconscious as shared inheritance rather than private property, structured by figures that recur across cultures. A reminder that the images a society uses to think about children, rescue, and danger are older than its policies.

  • Marie-Louise von Franz

    The Interpretation of Fairy Tales (1970)

    Jung's closest collaborator, and the more rigorous reader of the material: folk tales treated as evidence of how a culture narrates abandonment, adoption, and the child who is taken.

  • Antonio Gramsci

    Prison Notebooks (1929–1935)

    Hegemony: rule that works through consent and common sense rather than force, which is why the arrangements that produce inequality can be defended in good faith by the people they harm.

  • Frantz Fanon

    Black Skin, White Masks (1952); The Wretched of the Earth (1961)

    Colonial domination as a psychic as well as a material fact, taking Hegel's dialectic into a world where recognition is refused outright. The point where the Marxist line and the critical race line meet.

  • Stuart Hall

    “Encoding/Decoding” (1973); Policing the Crisis (1978)

    Race as the modality in which class is lived, and moral panic as the mechanism by which a state manufactures the crisis it then claims to answer. The closest model for reading how child welfare reform gets talked about.

  • Michael Burawoy

    Manufacturing Consent (1979); “For Public Sociology” (2005)

    Consent produced on the shop floor rather than imposed on it, and the extended case method as a way of moving between a single site and the structure it sits in.

  • Henri Lefebvre

    The Production of Space (1974)

    Space as produced rather than given — a container that policy fills unevenly. Why a single national policy can land as several different policies depending on where a child happens to live.

  • Erik Olin Wright

    Classes (1985); Envisioning Real Utopias (2010)

    Class locations specified precisely enough to be measured, and the insistence that describing a structure is only half the job — the other half is showing that it could be built otherwise.

  • Charles Tilly

    Durable Inequality (1998)

    Inequality durable because it is installed in categories — paired and unequal — rather than carried in individuals. Exploitation and opportunity hoarding are organizational mechanisms, which means they are built and can be built otherwise.

  • W. E. B. Du Bois

    The Philadelphia Negro (1899); Black Reconstruction (1935)

    Empirical sociology in the service of a structural argument, decades before the discipline claimed the method as its own. The color line as a product of arrangements rather than a fact about the people it divides.

  • Derrick Bell

    “Brown v. Board and the Interest-Convergence Dilemma” (1980)

    Interest convergence: reform arrives when the interests of the powerful happen to align with the interests of the harmed, and recedes when they part. A way of reading agreement between opposed camps without mistaking it for a shared aim.

  • Kimberlé Crenshaw

    “Demarginalizing the Intersection” (1989)

    Intersectionality as an account of how single-axis categories fail the people who sit between them — a problem of institutional record-keeping as much as of theory.

  • Cheryl Harris

    “Whiteness as Property” (1993)

    Whiteness analyzed as a property interest the law protects: an expectation of advantage that becomes a settled entitlement, and so a thing that can be defended without ever being named.

  • Patricia Hill Collins

    Black Feminist Thought (1990)

    The matrix of domination, and the epistemic claim beneath it: those moved through a system know things about it that its administrators cannot see from where they stand.

  • Saidiya Hartman

    Scenes of Subjection (1997); Lose Your Mother (2007)

    The afterlife of slavery, and critical fabulation as a way of writing about lives that survive only in the ledgers of the institutions that harmed them. The problem of the archive, for anyone working from administrative records.

  • Dorothy Roberts

    Shattered Bonds (2002); Torn Apart (2022)

    The child welfare system read as a family policing system, continuous with other carceral institutions rather than exceptional to them. The nearest theoretical neighbor to work on foster care and privatization.

  • Ruth Wilson Gilmore

    Golden Gulag (2007)

    Organized abandonment, and racism as the state-sanctioned production of vulnerability to premature death. The sharpest available answer to how an institution comes to treat an outcome as an acceptable loss rather than a design failure.

  • Roy Bhaskar

    A Realist Theory of Science (1975); The Possibility of Naturalism (1979)

    Critical realism: the world is stratified, and generative mechanisms are real whether or not they show up in the regularities we happen to observe. Grounds for saying a structure exists without having to see it directly.

  • Margaret Archer

    Realist Social Theory (1995); Structure, Agency and the Internal Conversation (2003)

    Analytical dualism: structure and agency operate over different time-scales and must be held apart to see how either works. Her quarrel with habitus is a quarrel about whether people deliberate their way through the systems that sort them.
