Theoretical Inspirations

This page is a map rather than a lineage. It has no root and no first entry — each visit opens somewhere different, in the middle of things. Follow a line to see what it connects; cut one to see what grows in its place.

“Any point of a rhizome can be connected to anything other, and must be.” — Deleuze & Guattari, A Thousand Plateaus

The Plateaus

  • Gilles Deleuze & Félix Guattari

    A Thousand Plateaus (1980)

    The rhizome against the tree: knowledge organized as a burrow with many entrances rather than a trunk with branches. Their distinction between the map and the tracing — between something you make and something you copy — is the form of this page.

  • Michel Foucault

    Discipline and Punish (1975); Society Must Be Defended (1976)

    Power as productive rather than merely prohibitive: it makes categories, records, and kinds of people rather than only forbidding things. The claim that institutions produce the populations they claim to merely sort begins here.

  • Pierre Bourdieu

    Distinction (1979); “The Forms of Capital” (1986)

    Capital in its economic, social, and cultural forms, converting into one another as it passes through institutions. Symbolic violence names the moment a contingent sorting comes to feel like a description of what people simply are.

  • Charles Tilly

    Durable Inequality (1998)

    Inequality durable because it is installed in categories — paired and unequal — rather than carried in individuals. Exploitation and opportunity hoarding are organizational mechanisms, which means they are built and can be built otherwise.

  • Geoffrey Bowker & Susan Leigh Star

    Sorting Things Out (1999)

    Classification systems as infrastructure: powerful precisely because they become invisible, and consequential for anyone who does not fit a box. What a case file can record is what an institution can see.

  • Ian Hacking

    “Making Up People” (1986)

    Looping effects: a classification changes the people classified, who then change the classification. Administrative categories are not descriptions applied after the fact but forces in the lives they name.

  • W. E. B. Du Bois

    The Philadelphia Negro (1899); Black Reconstruction (1935)

    Empirical sociology in the service of an argument about structure, decades before the discipline claimed it. The insistence that racial disparity is produced by arrangements and not by the populations measured.

  • Derrick Bell

    “Brown v. Board and the Interest-Convergence Dilemma” (1980)

    Interest convergence: reform arrives when the interests of the powerful happen to align with the interests of the harmed, and recedes when they part. A way of reading agreement between opposed camps without mistaking it for a shared aim.

  • Kimberlé Crenshaw

    “Demarginalizing the Intersection” (1989)

    Intersectionality as an account of how single-axis categories fail the people who sit between them — a problem of institutional record-keeping as much as of theory.

  • Dorothy Roberts

    Shattered Bonds (2002); Torn Apart (2022)

    The child welfare system read as a family policing system, continuous with other carceral institutions rather than exceptional to them. The nearest theoretical neighbor to work on foster care and privatization.

  • Ruth Wilson Gilmore

    Golden Gulag (2007)

    Organized abandonment, and racism as the production of vulnerability to premature death. The sharpest available answer to how an institution comes to treat an outcome as an acceptable loss rather than a design failure.

  • Loïc Wacquant

    Punishing the Poor (2009)

    The welfare and penal arms of the state as a single apparatus for managing poverty, one hand supervising what the other withdraws. Where Bourdieu's field theory meets the administration of the poor.

  • Patricia Hill Collins

    Black Feminist Thought (1990)

    The matrix of domination, and the epistemic claim beneath it: those moved through a system know things about it that its administrators cannot see from where they stand.

  • Henri Lefebvre

    The Production of Space (1974)

    Space as produced rather than given — a container that policy fills unevenly. Why a single national policy can land as several different policies depending on where a child happens to live.

  • Doreen Massey

    Space, Place and Gender (1994)

    Power-geometry: the same process places people differently, and mobility for some is built from immobility for others. The spatial companion to categorical inequality.
